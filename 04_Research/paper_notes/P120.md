# P120 — Subrahmanyam (2010): The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns: 25 Years of Research

**Citation:** Subrahmanyam, A. (2010). The cross-section of expected stock returns: What have we learnt from the past twenty-five years of research? *European Financial Management*, 16(1), 27–42.

---

## Core Thesis

A broad review of 25 years of cross-sectional return predictability research (1985-2010). Organizes predictors into four categories: (1) informal Wall Street wisdom, (2) theoretical risk-return models, (3) behavioral biases, and (4) frictions (illiquidity, short-sale constraints). Identifies unresolved methodological issues and makes the case that the field's progress is hampered by inadequate cross-talk between research groups and insufficient attention to correlational structure among predictors (50+ variables documented).

The section on illiquidity (Section 5) synthesizes the key papers through 2009 and places them in context of the broader factor zoo literature.

---

## Factor Construction (Review Summary)

**Summary of illiquidity predictors documented as of 2010:**

| Paper | Measure | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Amihud-Mendelson (1986) | Bid-ask spread | Higher spread → higher return; significant |
| Brennan-Subrahmanyam (1996) | Kyle lambda (GH model) | Lambda positively related to returns |
| Datar et al. (1998) | Turnover rate | Turnover negatively related to returns |
| Amihud (2002) | ILLIQ = |ret|/vol | Positive premium, time-series and cross-section |
| Pastor-Stambaugh (2003) | Gamma reversal | Liquidity beta → 7.5%/year premium |
| Liu (2006) | LM12 (zero days + turnover) | Two-factor model outperforms FF3 |
| Chordia et al. (2008) | Kyle-theory derived | Positively related to expected returns |

**Methodological critique:** Many cross-sectional variables are correlated with each other, making it hard to determine which ones capture truly independent dimensions of expected return. The field needs:
1. Comprehensive multi-variable models
2. Consistent use of a standard set of controls
3. Robustness across regression vs. portfolio approaches
4. Out-of-sample validation

**On information measures:**
- Easley et al. (2002) PIN (Probability of Informed Trading): priced in cross-section
- Duarte-Young (2009): decompose PIN into informed component + liquidity component; only liquidity component priced
- Sadka (2006): time-variations in illiquidity parameter (Kyle-type) priced
- These suggest: the liquidity component (not asymmetric information per se) drives the return-friction link

**On short-sale constraints:**
- Jones-Lamont (2002): high borrow costs → low future returns
- Asquith et al. (2005): high short interest + low institutional ownership → low future returns
- Nagel (2005): high institutional ownership → less short-sale constrained → more efficient pricing

## Key Results

**Survey conclusions:**
1. Illiquidity is robustly related to expected returns across multiple measures and methods
2. The effect is strongest for small-cap stocks and microcaps
3. After controlling for size and B/M, the illiquidity effect is smaller but still significant
4. The distinction between LEVEL of illiquidity (characteristic) vs. RISK (beta) is important: both appear priced
5. Short-sale constraints act as frictions that create persistent mispricing (overvaluation of hard-to-short stocks)
6. The field needs better understanding of which dimension of illiquidity matters most

**Open questions highlighted:**
- How do we separate liquidity risk from momentum risk? (PS shows they're correlated)
- Is PIN really measuring informed trading or just a statistical artifact?
- Do illiquidity effects survive 2001 decimalization? (Anticipates Drienko et al. 2018's findings)
- How do time-varying betas affect illiquidity risk measurement?

## Actionable Implementation

```r
# Comprehensive liquidity factor composite
# Based on Subrahmanyam (2010) survey synthesis

compute_comprehensive_liquidity_score <- function(df) {
  # Combine multiple measures for robustness
  df %>%
    group_by(date) %>%
    mutate(
      # 1. Amihud ILLIQ (price impact)
      illiq = abs(ret) / (volume_krw / 1e8),
      illiq_rank = percent_rank(illiq),

      # 2. Turnover rate (Datar et al.)
      turn_rank = 1 - percent_rank(monthly_turnover),  # inverted: low turn = bullish

      # 3. Liu LM12 (zero days + turnover) — computed separately
      # lm12_rank = percent_rank(lm12),

      # 4. Composite score (equal-weight)
      liquidity_composite = (illiq_rank + turn_rank) / 2
    )
}

# Screen: exclude top 5% ILLIQ (most illiquid microcaps)
# Signal: liquidity_composite as factor score (high = illiquid = bullish)
# Note: per Robeco (P110), the signal is weaker for large-caps
```
