# P015: Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice (1980)

## Core Finding
Thaler introduces the concept of "mental accounting" — the cognitive process by which people organize, evaluate, and track financial activities. Key anomalies documented: underweighting of opportunity costs (sunk cost fallacy), failure to ignore sunk costs, "chooser's regret" leading to option value seeking, and reference-point-dependent utility consistent with prospect theory. Proposes replacing normative expected utility with a descriptive behavioral framework.

## Key Metrics
- Alpha/Return: N/A (behavioral economics theory paper)
- Statistical strength: Survey and questionnaire evidence from hundreds of subjects
- Sample: Non-economists; Cornell University, 1980; Journal of Economic Behavior and Organization

## Korea Applicability
HIGH — Mental accounting is ubiquitous in Korean retail investment behavior. The "house money effect" (being more risk-tolerant with gains), sunk cost fallacy (holding Korean conglomerate stocks after restructuring), and hedonic editing all create exploitable mispricings.

## Strategy Implication
Mental accounting creates the "house money effect": after market gains, investors become more risk-tolerant and chase momentum; after losses, they become risk-averse and create reversal. Signal construction: market-state-conditioned factor weights. In the macro regime framework, upward-trending market regimes should weight momentum factors more heavily; downward-trending regimes should weight value/mean-reversion factors more heavily. Also: sunk cost fallacy implies that companies with large prior capital investments (high capex history) are reluctant to divest poor assets — negative asset efficiency signal (asset turnover decline).
