# P094 — Market and Industry Factors in Stock Price Behavior

**Authors**: Benjamin F. King
**Source**: Journal of Business, Vol. 39, No. 1 (January 1966), pp. 139-190
**Year**: 1966
**Category**: Factor_Return_Articles / General_Factor_Articles
**File**: `factor_analysis_industries.pdf`

---

## 1. Core Thesis

King applies factor analysis to the covariance matrix of monthly stock returns for 63 NYSE securities across 6 SEC-classified industries (tobacco, petroleum, metals, railroads, utilities, stores) from June 1927 to December 1960. The central finding is that stock price changes can be decomposed into a market-wide factor and industry-specific factors, with the market factor explaining approximately 50-52% of the variance of a typical stock, and industry factors explaining an additional 10-13%. This paper establishes empirically that the covariance structure of returns has a hierarchical factor structure: market → industry → firm. It is the empirical foundation for BARRA-style multi-factor models and the conceptual basis for industry-neutralization in portfolio construction.

---

## 2. Factor Construction

**Data**: 63 NYSE common stocks across 6 two-digit SEC industries:
1. Tobacco products (10 stocks)
2. Petroleum products (11 stocks)
3. Metals (ferrous and non-ferrous) (11 stocks)
4. Railroads (10 stocks)
5. Utilities (10 stocks)
6. Retail stores (11 stocks)

June 1927 – December 1960 (403 months). Monthly first-differences of log prices (log returns). Adjusted for dividends, splits, rights.

**Factor analytic model**:
```
y_jt = mu_j + Lambda*f_t + u_jt

where:
  y_jt = monthly log price change for stock j at time t (J x 1 vector)
  Lambda = J x m factor loading matrix (fixed)
  f_t = m x 1 vector of common factor scores
  u_jt = unique (idiosyncratic) component
  Sigma = Lambda*E(f_t*f_t')*Lambda' + Phi (where Phi = diagonal specific variance matrix)
```

**Estimation approach**:
1. **Communality estimation**: R^2 from regressing each stock on all other 62 stocks. Mean communality R^2 = 0.72 over the full period.
2. **Market factor extraction**: Centroid method (equal-weighted average) and Guttman-Harris (minimum-variance, market-cap-weighted via reciprocal of uniqueness). Both give nearly identical first factor.
3. **Residual covariance**: After removing market factor, G_1 = G - lambda_1*lambda_1'. This residual matrix is analyzed for industry clustering.
4. **Cluster analysis**: Hierarchical linkage clustering on residual correlation matrix. Groups emerge corresponding to the 6 SEC industries.
5. **Multiple factor analysis**: Six industry factors extracted from G_1 in addition to market factor.

**Key metric**: Proportion of variance explained by each factor, by industry, across sub-periods.

---

## 3. Key Results

**Market factor**:
- Market factor (first centroid) explains on average 52% of variance of a typical stock
- First sub-period (1927-1935, Depression era): market factor explains ~58% of variance (higher comovement during crises)
- Last sub-period (1952-1960): market factor explains only ~31% of variance (declining comovement, more diversification)
- Mean R^2 for full period: 0.722 (communality estimate)

**Industry factors**:
- Industries with strongest industry effect: Petroleum, metals, rails (proportion of variance from industry = 11-13%)
- Weakest industry effect: Tobacco, stores (9-10%)
- Industry factor correlations: mostly negative between industries (inter-industry diversification is effective)
- When market factor is removed, stocks cluster by SEC two-digit industry in the cluster analysis (Figure 2)
- 7-factor model (market + 6 industries) explains ~86% of mean communality (Table 10, 0.863)

**Stationarity test**:
- Factor pattern relatively stable across sub-periods for industry factors (low variation)
- Market effect shows non-stationarity: declines from 1927 to 1960
- This decline in market factor R^2 is attributed to increasing diversification and fewer economy-wide shocks post-WWII

**Practical takeaway**:
- Figure 1 (residual covariance pattern): block-diagonal structure confirms industry comovement; off-diagonal blocks near zero — cross-industry residual covariance is negligible
- "Pass 52" in cluster analysis: stocks cluster exactly into 6 SEC industries

## 4. Korea / International Applicability

**Highly applicable for Korean factor model design**:
- Korean market has strong industry/sector concentration (chaebol-dominated sectors: semiconductors, autos, chemicals, financial)
- Market factor dominates even more in Korea than US: KOSPI has very high cross-sectional beta correlation during stress events (2008 GFC, 2020 COVID crash) — expect market factor R^2 > 60% in Korean covariance matrix
- **Industry factor importance in Korea**: Samsung Electronics (IT), Hyundai Motor (Autos), POSCO (Metals) each dominate their sectors, creating strong industry clustering
- Chaebol conglomerates span multiple industries — need to use Chaebol group as a factor, not just KRX sector
- The finding that comovement weakens over time suggests that Korean market (emerging → developed transition) will show decreasing market factor R^2, increasing industry diversification

**Portfolio construction implication**:
- Industry neutralization is validated: after removing market beta, residual covariance is primarily industry-driven. Industry-neutralizing a portfolio removes the single largest source of non-market common risk.
- For KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150 strategy: neutralize to GICS sector (11 sectors) to remove this structured comovement.

## 5. Actionable Implications for Pipeline

1. **Covariance matrix decomposition**: When building the Korean factor model, ensure the first factor (market) is extracted first, followed by industry factors. Do not use raw covariance matrix for portfolio optimization — the factor structure is too concentrated.

2. **Industry-neutralized residuals**: Before computing any alpha signal (BM, MOM, ROE), residualize returns against market + industry factors:
```r
# Industry-neutralized residual computation
neutralize_returns <- function(returns_panel, mkt_return, industry_dummies) {
  returns_panel %>%
    group_by(date) %>%
    do({
      model <- lm(ret ~ r_mkt + factor(sector), data = .)
      mutate(., resid_ret = residuals(model))
    })
}
```

3. **Korean comovement test**: Compute the mean R^2 from regressing each KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150 stock's returns on the remaining stocks. Compare to King's 0.72 benchmark. If higher, more aggressive factor neutralization is required.

4. **Temporal stability check**: Monitor whether market factor R^2 is declining in Korean data (as King found in US). If so, adjust factor model accordingly — more industry weight, less market weight.

## 6. Strategy Ideas Extracted

- **Industry-Relative Alpha Strategy**: Rank stocks within each KRX industry (or GICS sector) separately. Long top-quintile within each industry, short bottom-quintile. This eliminates industry beta and captures pure stock selection alpha. Directly motivated by King's finding that industry factors are the dominant residual source of comovement.
- **Market + Industry Factor Decomposition Signal**: Compute the "idiosyncratic drift" of each stock: cumulative residual return after removing market + industry effects over the past 3 months. This is a purer alpha signal than raw return momentum, free of systematic factor noise.
- **Sector Rotation Using Industry Factor Returns**: Estimate monthly industry factor returns (as in the King centroid model). Build a momentum signal on industry factor returns (not individual stock returns). Rotate into top-performing industry factors each month, weighted by recent factor return strength.
