# P154: Market Value, Time, and Risk (1961)
**Category:** Portfolio_Theory | **Priority:** 3
**Author:** Jack L. Treynor | **Original draft:** 8/8/61, revised 4/29/15 (minor edits by Craig William French)

## Core Contribution to Factor Investing
Treynor's 1961 manuscript (unpublished until French reprinted it in 2015) is historically the **first CAPM derivation**, predating Sharpe's 1964 publication. Treynor approached the problem from corporate finance rather than portfolio theory — asking how capital budgeting decisions should account for market risk. His three fundamental problems of investment decisions: (a) defining the decision and its cash flows, (b) determining the effect on market value, and (c) appraising the financial risk entailed. For factor investing: Treynor's corporate finance angle is the bridge between asset pricing theory and the fundamental factor signals (ROE, GPA, ICR) we use — these are precisely measures of how well management answers problems (b) and (c). A company with high ROE and low ICR is solving Treynor's problems well; the market should price this systematically.

## Key Formula/Result
The "experiment space" concept: Treynor characterized risk by mapping investment outcomes onto a two-dimensional space where:
- One axis represents the time-discounted expected value of cash flows
- The other axis represents the variance/covariance of those cash flows with the market

Key corporate valuation insight:
```
Market Value = f(Expected Cash Flows, Risk of Cash Flows, Time)
```
The objective of corporate management: maximize the price for which present owners can sell their interest = maximize present market value.

Risk-adjusted discount rate problem: Treynor showed that the common practice of using a single WACC to discount all projects is wrong — each project's discount rate must reflect its **own** systematic risk contribution to the firm's portfolio. This is equivalent to project-specific beta pricing.

## Modern Relevance for Korea Strategy
Treynor's corporate finance framing directly motivates why our fundamental factors (GPA = Gross Profit/Assets, ICR = Interest Coverage Ratio, Accrual) work: they measure quality of management's answers to Treynor's three problems. High GPA means the company efficiently translates assets into gross profit (problem b). Low Accrual means cash flows match reported earnings (problems a and c). Low ICR means dangerous financial risk (problem c). The Treynor framework also warns against using a single risk-free hurdle in our `hurdle_gate.R` — ideally each strategy's hurdle should reflect its specific factor risk loading. Our regime-conditional hurdle gates are a step in this direction.
