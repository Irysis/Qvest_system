# P098 — Stock Market Indices: A Principal Components Analysis

**Authors**: George J. Feeney and Donald D. Hester
**Source**: Cowles Foundation Discussion Paper No. 175, Yale University, October 6, 1964
**Year**: 1964
**Category**: Factor_Return_Articles / General_Factor_Articles
**File**: `stock market indices a principal components analysis.pdf`

---

## 1. Core Thesis

Feeney and Hester apply principal components analysis (PCA) to the 30 Dow Jones Industrial stocks (1951-1963) to construct statistically optimal stock market indices and compare them to the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJI). The paper asks: what is the best linear combination of stock prices (or returns) to summarize market information? The answer: the first principal component — i.e., the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue of the price/return covariance matrix — is essentially equivalent to the DJI for purposes of tracking the overall market (R = 0.992). The DJI, despite its naive price-weighting, is a very good approximation to the optimal index. The paper also identifies a second component (perpendicular to the market) that discriminates between producer-goods and consumer-goods sectors.

## 2. Factor Construction

**Data**: 30 Dow Jones Industrial stocks, December 31, 1961 universe. 50 consecutive quarters from Q4 1950 to Q4 1963 (403 monthly observations). Adjusted for stock dividends and splits. Source: Wall Street Journal, Standard and Poor's Stock Guide.

**Three index variants**:

**Index a (raw price PCA)**:
- Input: end-of-quarter adjusted price levels X_t (30 x T matrix M)
- Covariance matrix: MM' (30 x 30)
- Extract largest eigenvalue lambda and corresponding eigenvector alpha
- Index: a_t = sum(alpha_i * X_it), normalized to sum(alpha_i^2) = lambda
- Component 1 accounts for 76% of generalized variance
- Component 2 accounts for 14%

**Index b (standardized price PCA)**:
- Input: standardized prices y_it = (X_it - X_bar_i) / s_i (zero mean, unit variance)
- Covariance matrix: correlation matrix of standardized prices
- Component 1 accounts for 66% of variance
- Component 2 accounts for 14%

**Index c (trend-adjusted standardized price PCA)**:
- Input: trend-adjusted z_it = (X_it - X_bar_i)/s_i - bt (removes time trend)
- First component accounts for only 40% of variance (trend removed)
- Reveals cyclical producer-consumer discrimination

**Mathematical basis**:
```
Find a = [alpha_1, ..., alpha_N] maximizing var(a' * X_t) = a'*MM'*a
subject to: a'*a = 1

Solution: a = eigenvector of MM' with largest eigenvalue lambda
```

**Key result — DJI comparison**:
- Index a, Component 1: R = 0.992 with DJI (Figure 1) — virtually identical tracking
- Index b, Component 1: R = 0.994 with DJI (Figure 3) — also nearly identical
- Index a, Component 2: R = -0.111 with DJI (Figure 2) — orthogonal, captures sector rotation
- DJI is effectively the first principal component of large-cap NYSE prices

## 3. Key Results

**Variance decomposition**:
| Index | Component 1 | Component 2 | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| a (raw price) | 75.76% | 13.93% | Dominated by high-price stocks (DuPont at $177) |
| b (standardized) | 65.67% | 13.70% | More equally weighted; time trend dominates PC1 |
| c (trend-adjusted) | 39.90% | 23.20% | Cyclical cross-section dominates |

**Factor loadings (Table 1 highlights)**:
- Index a, Component 1: DuPont (0.951), US Steel (0.407), Alcoa (0.405), GE (0.376) — high-price stocks dominate
- Index b, Component 1: All loadings positive and large (0.3-0.97) except Chrysler (-0.305) — market factor
- Index c, Component 2: AT&T (-0.773), Eastman Kodak (-0.779), General Foods (-0.841), Sears (-0.694) negative; Alcoa (+0.424), Bethlehem (+0.761) positive — consumer vs. producer sector split

**Consumer vs. producer sector**:
- Second component of indices a, b, c consistently separates consumer-oriented stocks (AT&T, Kodak, GF, P&G, Sears, Woolworth) from producer-goods stocks (metals, chemicals, industrials)
- This is the precursor to the modern industry factor in BARRA-style models

**Stationarity**: Correlation and covariance matrices are relatively stable across the four sub-periods, supporting use of fixed loadings over time.

## 4. Korea / International Applicability

**Conceptually foundational for KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150 index design**:
- The finding that a market-cap-weighted (or PCA-weighted) index is essentially equivalent to the first principal component applies to Korean market: KOSPI200 is well-approximated by PC1 of KOSPI200 constituent returns
- **Korean PCA structure**:
  - PC1: Market factor (dominated by Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix, LG Chem, etc.)
  - PC2: Likely captures semiconductor vs. consumer/domestic split
  - PC3: Possible KOSDAQ growth vs. KOSPI value split
- The "equal weighting vs. PCA weighting" debate is relevant for constructing the benchmark factor in our backtest. The PCA approach justifies value-weighting.

**Portfolio construction application**:
- Use PCA on KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150 return covariance matrix to:
  1. Extract the "pure market" factor (PC1) for beta estimation
  2. Identify secondary factors (PC2, PC3) that represent investable sector tilts
  3. Build a PCA-based factor model as an alternative to the fundamental BARRA approach

**Korea-specific insight**: If the Korean market's PC1 explains >70% of variance (likely, given chaebol concentration), a single-factor market model captures most of the covariance structure. Industry factors (PC2+) capture remaining systematic variation.

## 5. Actionable Implications for Pipeline

1. **PCA on Korean return data**:
```r
# PCA factor extraction for KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150
run_pca_factors <- function(returns_wide, n_factors = 5) {
  # returns_wide: T x N matrix of monthly returns
  cov_matrix <- cov(returns_wide, use = "pairwise.complete.obs")
  pca_result <- prcomp(returns_wide, scale = FALSE, center = TRUE)

  # Factor loadings (eigenvectors)
  loadings <- pca_result$rotation[, 1:n_factors]

  # Factor returns (scores)
  factor_returns <- pca_result$x[, 1:n_factors]

  # Variance explained
  var_explained <- (pca_result$sdev^2) / sum(pca_result$sdev^2)

  return(list(loadings = loadings, factor_returns = factor_returns,
              var_explained = var_explained))
}
```

2. **PCA vs. DJI comparison for KOSPI**: Compute correlation between KOSPI200 index and PC1 of KOSPI200 constituent returns. Expect R > 0.99 (as in Feeney-Hester for DJI). This validates using KOSPI200 as the market factor proxy.

3. **Sector rotation from PC2**: Monitor the sign and magnitude of the second principal component. Persistent positive or negative PC2 return indicates sector rotation (growth vs. value, domestic vs. export). Can use PC2 as a sector rotation signal.

4. **Dynamic PCA**: Re-estimate PCA covariance matrix with rolling 36-month window. Track stability of PC1 loadings over time. If loadings shift (e.g., Samsung's weight in PC1 increases), update factor model accordingly.

## 6. Strategy Ideas Extracted

- **PCA-Factor Orthogonal Alpha**: After extracting 3 principal components from KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150 returns, compute PC-orthogonal residuals for each stock. These residuals are by construction uncorrelated with all major market/sector factors. Use them as the input signal for stock selection — the purest form of the residual-first approach.
- **PC2 Sector Rotation Strategy**: Track the rolling 3-month return of the second principal component (which discriminates between domestic consumption stocks and export/industrial stocks in Korea). When PC2 is trending positive, overweight domestic consumer stocks; when negative, overweight exporters/industrials.
- **PCA-Based Index Replication / Smart Beta**: Construct a minimum-variance index of KOSPI200 stocks by weighting each stock by its PC1 loading (normalized). This is a principled alternative to market-cap weighting. Expected to track KOSPI200 with lower tracking error and lower specific risk concentration.
