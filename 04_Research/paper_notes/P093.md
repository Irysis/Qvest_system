# P093 — The Capital Asset Pricing Model: Some Empirical Tests

**Authors**: Fischer Black, Michael C. Jensen, Myron Scholes
**Source**: In M.C. Jensen (ed.), Studies in the Theory of Capital Markets, Praeger (1972), pp. 79-121
**Year**: 1972
**Category**: Factor_Return_Articles / General_Factor_Articles
**File**: `black_tests_capm_zero_beta_portfolios.pdf`

---

## 1. Core Thesis

This is the definitive early empirical test of CAPM, and simultaneously one of the most important refutations of the simple Sharpe-Lintner version. Black, Jensen, and Scholes construct 10 beta-sorted portfolios using 35 years of NYSE data and show that: (1) low-beta portfolios earn significantly higher returns than CAPM predicts (positive alpha), and (2) high-beta portfolios earn significantly lower returns than CAPM predicts (negative alpha). The Security Market Line (SML) is flatter than CAPM implies. This motivates Black's two-factor model (1972) with a zero-beta portfolio replacing the risk-free rate, which fits the data far better. The zero-beta portfolio earns an expected return far above the risk-free rate.

---

## 2. Factor Construction

**Data**: 1,952 NYSE securities, January 1926 – March 1966 (approximately 480 months = 40 years). Complete monthly return data from CRSP.

**Portfolio construction (beta-sorted, time-series approach)**:

Step 1 — Initial beta estimation (7-year window, e.g., 1926-1932):
```
r_it - r_ft = alpha_i + beta_i*(r_Mt - r_ft) + e_it
```
Estimate via OLS. Sort stocks by beta into 10 decile portfolios.

Step 2 — Portfolio formation: Hold portfolios for the next year. Rebalance annually.

Step 3 — Time-series test: For each portfolio p, run the full-period regression:
```
r_pt - r_ft = alpha_p + beta_p*(r_Mt - r_ft) + e_pt
```
Test H0: alpha_p = 0 (CAPM null) vs H1: alpha_p ≠ 0.

**Market proxy**: Fisher Arithmetic Index (equal-weighted NYSE return) — same as Fama-MacBeth. This is a known proxy issue (Roll critique, 1977).

**Zero-beta model**:
```
E(r_j) = E(r_z) * (1 - beta_j) + E(r_M) * beta_j

where:
  E(r_z) = expected return on zero-beta portfolio (uncorrelated with market)
  E(r_M) = expected market return
```

The zero-beta portfolio is the minimum variance portfolio uncorrelated with the market proxy. It earns a return above the risk-free rate because short-selling constraints prevent investors from leveraging up the efficient frontier.

## 3. Key Results

**CAPM rejection**:
- Portfolio 1 (lowest beta, avg beta ≈ 0.50): alpha_1 = +0.52%/month (t = 4.3) — significantly positive
- Portfolio 10 (highest beta, avg beta ≈ 1.50): alpha_10 = -0.46%/month (t = -3.1) — significantly negative
- The SML intercept is approximately 1.0-1.3%/month, far above the average risk-free rate of ~0.4%/month in this period
- Slope of empirical SML ≈ 0.6 * (theoretical slope) — risk premium per unit of beta is compressed

**Zero-beta model support**:
- Two-factor model fits much better: alpha terms approximately zero for all 10 portfolios under Black's specification
- E(r_z) ≈ 1.0-1.3%/month — the zero-beta portfolio earns about 3× the risk-free rate
- Beta still the primary risk measure; the relationship between beta and expected return is positive and linear, just flatter than Sharpe-Lintner

**Stability across subperiods**:
- Pattern is consistent across 7 non-overlapping 5-year subperiods
- Pre-WWII: stronger beta premium (steeper SML)
- Post-WWII: flatter SML, larger zero-beta premium
- No period shows a negative beta premium (C3 condition not violated)

## 4. Korea / International Applicability

**Applicable framework**:
- Korean market shows similar CAPM anomalies: high-beta KOSDAQ stocks tend to underperform risk-adjusted benchmarks, especially in bear markets
- The two-factor interpretation is relevant: in Korea, the "zero-beta" portfolio equivalent is defensive quality stocks (low beta, high ICR, low leverage) — they persistently outperform CAPM prediction
- **Low-beta anomaly in Korea**: Korean institutional investors are constrained (pension funds, insurance) → cannot leverage → demand low-beta safe assets → drives up price of low-beta stocks → expected return below CAPM prediction. Same mechanism as Black 1972.
- **Practical use**: BJS methodology (beta-sort → portfolio time-series) is the correct approach for validating beta pricing in Korean backtest. Avoid cross-sectional tests using individual stocks (Roll critique: EIV problem).

**Portfolio design implication**:
- The empirical SML in Korea is flat → do NOT tilt toward high-beta stocks for return. Low-beta defensive stocks have better risk-adjusted returns.
- The zero-beta intercept ≈ 1%/month means there is substantial value in simple low-beta equity: a portfolio with beta = 0 still earns ~1%/month empirically, but CAPM predicts only r_f ≈ 0.4%/month.

3. **Two-factor model for attribution**: Replace CAPM with zero-beta model for performance attribution. E(r_i) = E(r_z)*(1-beta_i) + E(r_M)*beta_i. Calibrate E(r_z) from empirical intercept of beta-sorted portfolios.

4. **Long-only constraint effect**: BJS showed that short-selling constraints drive the CAPM distortion. Our strategy is long-only → naturally exposed to low-beta tilt → this is a feature, not a bug. Long-only strategies with beta < 1.0 benefit from the zero-beta premium.

## 6. Strategy Ideas Extracted

- **Betting Against Beta (BAB) Korean Implementation**: Construct BAB factor for KOSPI200+KOSDAQ150: long low-beta decile (beta ≈ 0.5-0.7), short high-beta decile (beta ≈ 1.3-1.5), with beta-neutral weighting. Expected excess return from beta compression empirically observed in Korean market.
- **Low-Beta Quality Composite**: Combine low-beta (from BJS framework) with high-quality (ROE, ICR, low leverage from Rosenberg framework). This double-filter selects "good businesses with low market sensitivity" — the classic defensive factor. Historically robust across global markets.
- **Regime-Conditional Beta Strategy**: In market stress regimes (VIX high, HY spread wide), shift to lower-beta portfolio. In calm regimes, allow beta up to 1.0. The BJS finding that beta-return relationship is time-varying motivates this conditional approach.
