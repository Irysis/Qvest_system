# P008: Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms (1961)

## Core Finding
Ellsberg demonstrates experimentally that people systematically prefer known-probability gambles over unknown-probability gambles ("ambiguity aversion"), violating Savage's subjective expected utility axioms. The "Ellsberg Paradox" shows that unmeasurable uncertainty (Knightian uncertainty) produces systematically different behavior than measurable risk, even when the rational agent should be indifferent.

## Korea Applicability
MEDIUM — Ambiguity aversion is particularly relevant in Korean markets during policy uncertainty, regulatory change (e.g., FSC announcements), and geopolitical events (North Korea tensions). Stocks with high ambiguity (new listings, companies with opaque reporting) may be systematically underpriced due to aversion.

## Strategy Implication
Ambiguity aversion implies that stocks with higher information uncertainty are systematically underowned and potentially underpriced. Proxy for ambiguity using: (1) analyst coverage count (fewer analysts = higher ambiguity), (2) earnings forecast dispersion, (3) time since IPO. Low-ambiguity stocks (well-covered, stable earnings) may be overpriced relative to fair value, while high-ambiguity stocks with positive fundamentals may offer excess returns. This is the theoretical basis for the "earnings surprise" and "neglected firm" effects.
