# P020: Oskamp: Overconfidence in Case-Study Judgments (1965)

## Core Finding
Oskamp demonstrates that clinical psychologists' confidence in their judgments increases with the amount of information provided, but accuracy does not improve (and may decline). With minimal information, accuracy and confidence are roughly matched; as information increases, confidence rises sharply while accuracy plateaus. This "more information = more confidence but not more accuracy" pattern is the core overconfidence-in-expertise phenomenon.

## Key Metrics
- Alpha/Return: N/A (clinical psychology experiment)
- Statistical strength: N = 32 clinical psychologists and students; progressive information disclosure design
- Sample: Clinical assessment of psychiatric patient case, 1965

## Korea Applicability
MEDIUM — Relevant to equity analysts and fund managers: more analyst reports, more management meetings, more "information" does not necessarily improve stock selection accuracy, but increases conviction and position sizing errors. Overconfidence grows with information access.

## Strategy Implication
Overconfidence in information richness implies that analyst consensus with high coverage (many analysts, many reports) may be MORE overconfident and thus less accurate than low-coverage stocks. Contrary to conventional wisdom, high analyst coverage may be a negative signal for forward returns — not because analysts are wrong more often, but because their high confidence leads to less mean-reversion opportunity for surprises. The "neglected firm" anomaly (Arbel et al. 1983) is partly explained by this: low-coverage, low-confidence stocks are priced more cautiously and offer higher subsequent returns. Signal: inverse analyst coverage count as factor weight booster.

## References
Oskamp (1965), Journal of Consulting Psychology 29:3; Einhorn (1978 overconfidence in expert judgment); Arbel et al. (1983 neglected firm effect)
