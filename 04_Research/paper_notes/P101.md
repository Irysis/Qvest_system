# P101 — Amihud & Mendelson (1986): Asset Pricing and the Bid-Ask Spread

**Citation:** Amihud, Y., & Mendelson, H. (1986). Asset pricing and the bid-ask spread. *Journal of Financial Economics*, 17(2), 223–249.

---

## Core Thesis

The seminal paper establishing that illiquidity (measured by bid-ask spread) is priced in equilibrium. Expected asset returns are an increasing and **concave** function of the relative bid-ask spread. A clientele effect exists: investors with longer holding periods select assets with higher spreads, because the amortized transaction cost decreases with horizon. Net-of-cost returns increase with holding period for high-spread assets.

Key equilibrium result: the gross (market-observed) return on asset j is determined by its highest-valued use (shortest holding period investor willing to hold it):

    d_j / V_j* = min_{i=1,...,M} { r_i* + μ_i * S_j }

where r_i* is investor i's required spread-adjusted return, μ_i is the liquidation probability per unit time, and S_j is the relative spread. Returns increase with spread but at a decreasing rate (concavity).

---

## Factor Construction

- **Measure:** Relative bid-ask spread S_j = (Ask - Bid) / Ask
- **Data:** NYSE stocks, 1961–1980; actual quoted spreads from Stoll and Whaley
- **Test:** Portfolio sort by spread (7 portfolios), size-controlled regression
- **Model:** E(r_j) = f(S_j) where f is increasing and concave

Empirical findings:
- Average risk-adjusted returns increase monotonically from lowest spread (0.48% relative spread → 0.71% monthly excess) to highest spread (3.21% spread → 1.73% monthly excess)
- Concavity confirmed: slope of return-spread relationship decreases as spread rises
- Spread effect persists after controlling for firm size

---

## Key Results

| Spread Quintile | Avg Spread | Monthly Return |
|---|---|---|
| Lowest | 0.48% | 0.71% |
| 2 | 0.96% | 0.89% |
| 3 | 1.34% | 0.97% |
| 4 | 2.00% | 1.23% |
| Highest | 3.21% | 1.73% |

- Spread explains returns beyond size (spread is NOT just a size proxy)
- Clientele segmentation: rational sorting by holding horizon
- Market efficiency interpretation: the premium is compensation, not an anomaly
- Liquidity helps explain the equity premium puzzle (equities illiquid vs. Treasuries)

---

## Korea-Specific Implications

- Korean market has historically wide bid-ask spreads especially in KOSDAQ; this paper's framework applies directly
- Clientele effect may be stronger: Korean retail investors (short horizon) tend to hold large-cap liquid stocks; institutional/foreign investors (longer horizon) more willing to hold illiquid KOSDAQ names
- The concavity finding suggests diminishing marginal returns to illiquidity — avoid extreme microcaps where cost-adjusted returns may not compensate
- KOSDAQ150 universe overlap with Amihud illiquidity gradient is strategically usable as a screen

**Korea RAWDATA.parquet implementation:**

```r
# Monthly relative spread approximation from OHLCV

# Spread-based liquidity screen (use as filter, not signal):
# Exclude stocks in top 10% spread (most illiquid microcaps)
# Long illiquid quintile 2-4, avoiding extreme tail

spread_screen <- function(df) {
  df %>%
    group_by(date) %>%
    mutate(
      illiq_rank = percent_rank(illiq),
      pass = illiq_rank < 0.90  # exclude top decile
    )
}
```

**Strategic use:** This paper establishes the theoretical basis for using illiquidity as a characteristic (level) rather than a risk factor. Use as a universe screen combined with momentum/value signals.
