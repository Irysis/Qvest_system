# P081: Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability? (McLean & Pontiff 2016)
**Category:** Anomaly_Research / Publication_Effect
**Relevance to Korea pipeline:** High

## Core Claim
Stock return predictability declines after academic publication, with the decline attributable to two distinct mechanisms: (1) statistical bias/data mining in the original study (~26% of total decay), and (2) market learning through arbitrage activity (~32% minimum — the "publication effect"). The publication effect is evidenced by increased trading volume, short interest, and cross-predictor correlation in post-publication periods.

## Key Findings (specific percentages, survival rates, which anomalies)
- **97 return predictors** studied from published academic papers
- Three-period framework:
  - **In-sample** (paper's original estimation window): 100% baseline return (by construction)
  - **Out-of-sample pre-publication**: declines to ~85% (statistical bias = ~15-26%)
  - **Post-publication**: declines to ~42% (total decline = 58%)
- **58% average post-publication decline** in predictor returns
- Upper bound on **statistical bias: ~26%** (data snooping, look-ahead, microcap effects)
- Minimum **publication/arbitrage effect: ~32%** (market learning after publication)
- More cited papers → larger post-publication decline (arbitrageurs pay attention to influential work)
- Table VI (trading activity post-publication):
  - Trading volume: **+18.7%**
  - Dollar volume: **+9.7%**
  - Return variance: **-6.5%** (lower noise = more efficient pricing)
  - Short interest: **+0.315%** (approximately **3x increase** from pre-publication level)
- Table VII: Post-publication predictor returns become correlated with OTHER published predictors (not unpublished ones) — consistent with arbitrageurs trading published signals together
- Anomalies in **high-IVOL / costly arbitrage stocks decay LESS** post-publication
- Larger anomaly return pre-publication → larger decay (higher return signals more mispricing to be corrected)

## Factors That SURVIVE Post-Publication
- Anomalies in **high idiosyncratic volatility (IVOL) stocks** — arbitrage is too risky/costly
- Anomalies in **illiquid / small cap** stocks — arbitrage is too costly
- Anomalies with **strong theoretical foundations** (harder to dismiss as data mining)
- **Low Vol / Min Volatility** factor: by definition selects stocks where arbitrage works well, but factor itself remains intact
- Anomalies in **real investment variables** (investment-to-assets, net operating assets): less affected by arbitrage flows per Akbas et al.

## Factors That DECAY Post-Publication
- Anomalies that were most highly cited and widely disseminated
- Anomalies in **liquid, low-IVOL large cap stocks** (easy to arbitrage away)
- Accrual-based anomalies (highly cited, widely known since Sloan 1996)
- Net stock issues (widely known, liquid stocks)
- **Short-side anomalies** more affected (short interest increase post-publication confirms)

## Korea/International Applicability
- Cross-reference with Jacobs & Muller (P079): US is the ONLY market with reliable post-publication decline
- Korea implication: **post-publication decay is primarily a US phenomenon**
- Korean quantitative arbitrage capital is far smaller than US → less publication-driven decay
- The 58% US average decay is NOT the right baseline for Korea
- Korea-specific estimate: probably 10-20% post-publication decay at most (based on international evidence)
- High-IVOL Korean stocks (mid-cap KOSDAQ) should preserve anomaly returns longer
- Short selling restrictions in Korea further reduce post-publication arbitrage (short interest mechanism disabled)

## Actionable Implications for Our Pipeline
1. **Do NOT blindly apply US decay corrections** to Korean factor returns
2. **Prioritize factors with strong theory** (decay-resistant): profitability (Novy-Marx theory), investment (q-theory), value (risk-based or behavioral)
3. **Short selling friction in Korea** is a structural advantage — anomaly returns persist longer on short side
4. For factor selection: prefer factors published BEFORE 2005 (more arbitrage capital already deployed) vs recently discovered ones (still being exploited)
5. **IVOL as regime signal**: in high-IVOL regimes, anomaly premia are larger AND more persistent
6. Build factor portfolio with **high-IVOL tilt** within KOSDAQ150 component
7. The ~42% residual return post-publication in US likely understates Korean factor strength
8. **Monitor factor crowding** via cross-predictor correlation (Table VII methodology): if our factors start correlating with each other more than expected, it signals crowding
