# P116 — Pastor & Stambaugh (2019): Liquidity Risk After 20 Years

**Citation:** Pastor, L., & Stambaugh, R.F. (2019). Liquidity risk after 20 years. *Critical Finance Review*, 8(1-2), 277–299. (Dated May 6, 2019)

---

## Core Thesis

Pastor and Stambaugh's **response** to two replication studies (Li-Novy-Marx-Velikov 2017 and Pontiff-Singla 2019) commissioned by the Critical Finance Review. Overall verdict: the liquidity risk premium is **alive and well**. Out-of-sample performance is even stronger than in-sample, confirming that the 2003 result was not a fluke. They defend their construction choices and clarify several methodological points including the critical zero-volume-day exclusion.

---

## Factor Construction

**PS (2003) regression (re-stated with clarification):**

    r^e_{i,d+1,t} = θ_{i,t} + φ_{i,t} * r_{i,d,t} + γ_{i,t} * sign(r^e_{i,d,t}) * v_{i,d,t} + ε_{i,d+1,t}

**CRITICAL CLARIFICATION revealed in this paper:**
- Original 2003 paper inadvertently omitted mention of the **v_{i,d,t} > 0** screen
- Without excluding zero-volume days, the PS regression is misspecified because on zero-volume days, the reported stock price is the bid-ask midpoint — which creates artificial positive correlation between r_{i,d,t} (the lagged control) and the signed-volume term, biasing γ estimates
- Without the screen: correlation between replication series and original is only 27-39%
- With the screen: correlation > 99%

**Out-of-sample performance:**
- In-sample (1968-1999): Carhart 4-factor alpha = 0.37%/month (t = 2.14)
- Out-of-sample (2000-2015): Carhart 4-factor alpha = **0.68%/month** (t = 2.31)
- The 2008 financial crisis: PS factor had negative innovation in 17 of 22 months between July 2007 and April 2009; three of the five largest drops correspond to Lehman Brothers, Bear Stearns, and the quant crisis — precisely when one would expect a liquidity factor to register low values

**Liquidity factor vs. alternatives:**
- PS factor correlation with bid-ask spread (1962-2017): only 29.6% — they capture different liquidity dimensions
- Bid-ask spread = static, point-in-time cost for retail investors
- PS gamma = dynamic, volume-related price impact for institutional investors
- Neither dominates; both may matter depending on investor type

**Modification analysis:**
- Value-weighted index: less appealing (large stocks dominate, losing diversification of estimation error in γ)
- Including zero-volume days: misspecified, biased
- Zero-intercept restriction: slightly weaker performance, possibly misspecified
- Including low-price stocks (< $5): problematic (one-tick moves dominate return variation)

**Pre-1962 evidence:**
- Pontiff-Singla (2019) extends to 1932-1967; premium is -2.72% per year, t = -1.36
- PS argue: pre-1962 data was NYSE only (larger stocks), post-1962 includes AMEX (smaller stocks); fundamentally different universes
- Inconclusive for determining whether premium is regime-dependent

---

## Key Results

1. PS aggregate liquidity measure successfully replicated (r = 0.99 correlation)
2. Out-of-sample premium 0.68%/month > in-sample 0.37%/month — premium increased after publication
3. 2008 crisis provides compelling out-of-sample validation
4. Modifications that exclude the PS methodology produce non-liquidity-like series (fail visual inspection)
5. Monthly rebalancing vs. annual: monthly yields 0.36-0.45%/year (t = 2.24-2.84); annual slightly stronger but both significant across all factor models (full sample 1968-2015)
6. PS factor accounts for half of momentum profits (momentum alpha drops by half when PS factor is included)

## Korea-Specific Implications

- The 2008 crisis validation is directly relevant to Korean market: Korean liquidity dried up severely in Fall 2008 and this should be reflected in aggregate Korean gamma measure
- The zero-volume day exclusion is even more critical for Korean implementation (KOSDAQ stocks frequently have zero-volume days)
- The "visual inspection" criterion for aggregate liquidity series is useful: a proper Korean aggregate liquidity series should show drops in Oct 1997, Aug 1998, Fall 2008, March 2020
- Monthly rebalancing yielding significant (if slightly weaker) alpha suggests monthly rebalancing is feasible for Korean implementation

Key Korean implementation notes from P116:

```r
# Critical filters for PS gamma estimation in Korea:
# 1. Exclude v_{i,d,t} = 0 (MANDATORY per PS's own clarification)
# 2. Exclude stocks with price < 1000 KRW or > 1,000,000 KRW (analog to $5-$1000 filter)
# 3. Use equal-number decile sorts (not market-cap weighted)
# 4. Annual December rebalancing for traded factor
# 5. Require >= 15 trading days per month for gamma estimation

# Korean aggregate liquidity time-series validation checklist:
check_liquidity_series <- function(gamma_ts) {
  crisis_months <- c("1997-10", "1997-11", "1998-08",
                     "2008-09", "2008-10", "2008-11",
                     "2020-03")
  crisis_vals <- gamma_ts[gamma_ts$ym %in% crisis_months, "avg_gamma"]
  normal_median <- median(gamma_ts$avg_gamma, na.rm = TRUE)

  # Crisis months should be below median (i.e., more negative gamma = less liquid)
  cat("Crisis months avg_gamma vs. normal median:\n")
  cat("Crisis:", mean(crisis_vals, na.rm = TRUE), "\n")
  cat("Normal median:", normal_median, "\n")
  cat("Ratio:", mean(crisis_vals, na.rm = TRUE) / normal_median, "\n")
  # Should be > 1 in magnitude (crisis = more negative = larger absolute value)
}
```

**Portfolio implication:** High-beta-to-PS-factor stocks should earn ~8%/year premium in normal times but suffer severely during crises. Design the portfolio to EITHER harvest this premium OR hedge it — but understand what you own.
