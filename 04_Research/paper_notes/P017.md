# P017: The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice (1981)

## Core Finding
Tversky & Kahneman demonstrate systematic preference reversals when identical decision problems are framed differently in terms of gains vs. losses. The "Asian Disease Problem" shows that a majority choose the certain outcome when framed as saving lives (risk aversion) but the risky option when framed as deaths (risk seeking) — despite the problems being mathematically equivalent. Preferences depend on the decision frame, not objective outcomes.

## Key Metrics
- Alpha/Return: N/A (behavioral decision theory)
- Statistical strength: N = 152 and N = 155 in two experiments; highly significant preference reversals
- Sample: Stanford University and University of British Columbia students; Science 1981

## Korea Applicability
HIGH — Framing effects are pervasive in Korean market behavior. Earnings reports framed as "beat by X won" vs. "revenue grew X%" produce different investor reactions. IPO marketing uses gain framing. Dividend announcements framed as "special dividend" vs. "regular dividend" are valued differently.

## Strategy Implication
Framing effects create post-announcement drift when the market initially reacts to the frame rather than the substance. Signal: companies that report earnings beats framed in revenue terms (not EPS) may be underreacted to — follow-through momentum. Also: stocks in "loss frames" (below 52-week high) are systematically treated with more risk-seeking behavior by their holders, creating volatility clusters around 52-week low levels. 52-week-high proximity as a signal (George & Hwang 2004) is a direct application of framing theory.
