# P022: Overconfidence 1975 — Miller and Ross (1975)

## Core Finding
Miller & Ross document the "self-serving attribution bias": people attribute successes to their own ability (internal attribution) and failures to external circumstances (external attribution). This asymmetric attribution maintains and enhances overconfidence even in the face of negative feedback — losses are blamed on bad luck, not skill, so confidence is not updated downward appropriately.

## Key Metrics
- Alpha/Return: N/A (social psychology attribution theory)
- Statistical strength: Experimental studies with performance feedback manipulation
- Sample: US psychology students, 1975

## Korea Applicability
HIGH — Self-serving attribution is extremely common in Korean investment culture. Retail investors who lose money on individual stocks attribute losses to market manipulation or bad luck rather than poor selection, maintaining overconfidence and continuing to trade actively. This is a key driver of the high Korean retail turnover documented empirically.

## Strategy Implication
Self-serving attribution sustains overconfident trading, which sustains the volume anomaly and momentum effects in Korean markets. The implication for factor model construction: high-turnover retail stocks (high retail volume / total volume ratio) represent overconfident investors who will eventually face performance drag. Use the turnover anomaly signal as a negative factor: high turnover predicts lower subsequent returns. In R: turnover_ratio = volume / shares_outstanding; abnormal_retail_turnover = signal from OHLCVS volume data. Short stocks with high abnormal retail turnover.
