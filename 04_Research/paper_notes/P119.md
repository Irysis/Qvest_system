# P119 — Drienko, Smith & von Reibnitz (2018): A Review of the Return-Illiquidity Relationship

**Citation:** Drienko, J., Smith, T., & von Reibnitz, A. (2018). A review of the return-illiquidity relationship. *Critical Finance Review* (January 2018 working paper version).

---

## Core Thesis

A careful replication and extension of **Amihud (2002)** showing that while the cross-sectional illiquidity premium is replicable and robust, the **time-series relationship deteriorates sharply post-publication** (after 1996). The paper argues this decay reflects genuine structural change (market microstructure improvements, decimalization, algorithmic trading) rather than data snooping. Critically, they find the ILLIQ ratio's empirical power comes primarily from its **denominator (dollar volume)** rather than its numerator (absolute return).

---

## Factor Construction

**Amihud (2002) ILLIQ measure:**

    ILLIQ_{i,y} = (1/D_{i,y}) * Σ_d |ret_{i,d}| / vol_{krw,i,d}

where D_{i,y} = number of trading days for stock i in year y.

**Filters (matching Amihud 2002):**
- NYSE stocks, January 1963 to December 1997 (in-sample); 1997-2014 (out-of-sample)
- Listing at end of year y-1 with > 200 days of return/volume data in year y
- Price > $5 at end of year y-1
- Market cap data available
- 1% trim of top and bottom ILLIQ distribution

**Cross-sectional test:** Fama-MacBeth of monthly returns on lagged ILLIQ_MA (market-adjusted ILLIQ), SIZE, SDRET, DIVYLD, BETA, R100, R100YR

**ILLIQ_MA = ILLIQ_iy / AILLIQ_y** (ratio to market average, as in Amihud 2002)

**In-sample ILLIQ descriptive statistics:**
- Mean annual ILLIQ: 0.278 (replication) vs. 0.337 (original)
- Out-of-sample (1997-2014): mean ILLIQ drops to 0.030 (10x lower!) due to HFT, decimalization
- Maximum in-sample SIZE: $2,235M; minimum out-of-sample SIZE: $2,834M

---

## Key Results

**In-sample replication (1963-1996):**
- ILLIQ_MA coefficient: 0.145 (t = 5.74) vs. original 0.162 (t = 6.55) — closely reproduced
- Positive significant cross-sectional relationship confirmed

**Out-of-sample (1997-2015):**
- Cross-sectional ILLIQ_MA coefficient: still positive and significant, but **markedly smaller**
- Time-series tests: **lose statistical significance and monotonic properties**
- ILLIQ values near zero post-2003 (driven by high-frequency trading increasing volume dramatically)

**Change-point analysis (k-fold cross-validation):**
- Only 16.9% of random test sets produce larger prediction error than the out-of-sample period (1997-2015)
- Confirms a structural change point around 1996-1997 (end of Amihud's original sample)
- Post-publication, investor sensitivity to illiquidity risk declined

**Key alternative interpretation:**
- ILLIQ ratio driven by **denominator** (dollar volume), not numerator (|ret|)
- Using 1/volume alone as illiquidity measure produces results "analogous in terms of sign, magnitude and significance" to ILLIQ
- ILLIQ = |ret| / volume; the |ret| term adds noise but the denominator drives results
- This means: dollar volume is the primary carrier of illiquidity information

**Why the decay?**
- Technological innovations: online brokerage, algorithmic trading → 90x increase in daily transactions (1993-2008 per Chordia et al. 2011)
- Decimalization (2001): reduced tick sizes, compressed bid-ask spreads
- Result: stocks that were illiquid in 1970s-1980s are now highly liquid → ILLIQ = 0.030 (down from 0.278)

---

## Korea-Specific Implications

- This paper's finding is directly relevant to Korean implementation: Korean market has also experienced massive liquidity improvement (Naver/Kakao era → algorithmic trading → compressed spreads)
- Need to check whether ILLIQ has structural change point in Korean data
- **The denominator insight is actionable**: use **inverse dollar volume (1/volume_krw)** as a cleaner liquidity signal — avoids the noise from absolute returns
- Korean market: decimalization equivalent was the shift to electronic order matching (2002-2006 period)
- Cross-sectional ILLIQ premium may still be significant in Korea even if time-series premium has decayed (as found in this paper)
